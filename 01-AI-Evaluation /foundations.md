# AI Fundamentals and Generative AI — Learning Synthesis

## Purpose

This learning synthesis documents foundational knowledge developed through introductory coursework in Artificial Intelligence (AI), Machine Learning (ML), Deep Learning, Generative AI, data, and responsible AI.

The purpose is to establish a foundation for progressing into AI evaluation, AI quality assurance, AI safety/assurance, and AI governance.

## 1. Understanding Artificial Intelligence

Artificial Intelligence refers broadly to systems and technologies designed to perform tasks that ordinarily require aspects of human intelligence.

AI can be applied to areas such as:

* understanding and generating language;
* recognizing patterns;
* making predictions;
* processing information;
* supporting decision-making; and
* automating tasks.

AI systems depend heavily on data, algorithms, models, computing resources, and the context in which they are deployed.

## 2. Machine Learning

Machine Learning is a major approach within AI in which systems learn patterns from data and use those patterns to make predictions, classifications, or other outputs.

### Supervised Learning

In supervised learning, a model learns from examples where the desired outcome or label is available.

The model uses training examples to learn relationships between inputs and expected outputs and can subsequently apply those learned patterns to new data.

### Key consideration

The quality and representativeness of training data can affect model performance.

This is important for AI evaluation because poor, incomplete, biased, or unrepresentative data can contribute to poor model performance.

## 3. Deep Learning and Neural Networks

Deep Learning is a subset of Machine Learning that uses neural networks with multiple layers to learn complex patterns from data.

Neural networks are composed of interconnected computational units that process information and learn patterns during training.

Deep learning can be used for tasks involving:

* image recognition;
* language processing;
* speech;
* pattern recognition; and
* other complex prediction or generation tasks.

A high-level understanding of these concepts is important for an AI evaluator because evaluation requires understanding what a model is designed to do and where model limitations may originate.

## 4. Generative AI

Generative AI refers to AI systems capable of generating new content based on patterns learned from data.

Generated outputs can include:

* text;
* images;
* audio;
* code; and
* other forms of content.

Generative AI can support activities such as writing, reading, summarization, communication, brainstorming, and other knowledge-work tasks.

### Generative versus Classification Systems

A classification system generally assigns an input to a category or predicts a label.

A generative system produces new content based on learned patterns.

Understanding this distinction is useful when evaluating an AI system because evaluation criteria should reflect the type of task the system is expected to perform.

## 5. Large Language Models

Large Language Models (LLMs) are AI models designed to process and generate human language.

At a high level, an LLM learns statistical patterns from large amounts of text and uses those learned patterns to generate responses to prompts.

However, the ability to generate fluent language does not automatically mean that every generated response is factually accurate, appropriate, unbiased, or safe.

This distinction is particularly important for AI evaluation.

## 6. Data and AI

Data is a fundamental input into many AI and Machine Learning systems.

Important considerations include:

* how data is acquired;
* the quality of the data;
* whether the data adequately represents the intended population or use case;
* how data is processed; and
* how data is used to train or operate AI systems.

Data-related weaknesses can affect model performance and may contribute to inaccurate or unfair outcomes.

For an AI evaluator, this means that a model output should sometimes be considered in relation to the data and context behind the system rather than evaluated only at the surface level.

## 7. AI Applications and Implementation

AI can be introduced into organizations through approaches such as:

* identifying appropriate use cases;
* conducting pilot projects;
* developing internal AI capability;
* exploring external and internal solutions;
* developing an AI strategy; and
* progressively integrating AI into organizational processes.

Implementation should consider not only technical capability but also organizational needs, risks, users, resources, and responsible use.

## 8. Responsible AI

Responsible AI focuses on developing and using AI systems in ways that address potential risks and promote appropriate outcomes.

Key principles identified in the coursework include:

### Fairness

AI systems should be assessed for potential unfair or discriminatory outcomes.

### Safety

AI systems should be designed and evaluated to reduce foreseeable harm and unsafe behavior.

### Privacy

Personal and sensitive information should be appropriately protected.

### Transparency

Users and relevant stakeholders should have an appropriate understanding of how AI systems are being used and what their limitations are.

### Accountability

There should be clear responsibility for the development, deployment, monitoring, and use of AI systems.

### Human Oversight

Human involvement remains important, particularly where AI outputs can affect people or consequential decisions.

## 9. AI Limitations

An important lesson from the coursework is that AI systems have limitations.

These may include:

* incorrect outputs;
* limitations arising from training data;
* difficulty handling unfamiliar contexts;
* biased or incomplete outputs;
* inappropriate recommendations;
* misunderstanding of user instructions; and
* other failures arising from the model or its deployment context.

Therefore:

> A fluent AI response should not automatically be treated as a correct response.

This principle will become central to subsequent AI evaluation exercises.

## 10. Connection to AI Evaluation

The foundational concepts above provide the basis for evaluating AI systems systematically.

An AI evaluator needs to consider questions such as:

1. Did the model understand the instruction?
2. Did it provide a relevant response?
3. Is the information factually accurate?
4. Does the response contain unsupported claims or hallucinations?
5. Is the response appropriate for the intended context?
6. Does it demonstrate bias or unfair treatment?
7. Does it create safety or privacy concerns?
8. Does it follow the required format or constraints?
9. Is the response useful to the intended user?
10. How severe is any identified failure?

The next stage of training will turn these questions into a formal evaluation rubric.
